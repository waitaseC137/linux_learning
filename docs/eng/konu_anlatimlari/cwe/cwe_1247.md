# 👾 CWE-1247 — Improper Protection Against Voltage and Clock Glitches

> **Voltage and clock glitches.** The device does not notice, and does not guard
> against, a momentary disturbance on its supply voltage or its clock signal. That
> single moment is enough to make the circuit **produce a wrong result**.

## NandGame's Second Silent Assumption

In [CWE-1300](./cwe_1300.md) we broke one of the game's assumptions: a gate spends
no energy. Here is the second one:

> **A gate always gives the right answer.**

While deriving every gate from NAND in lesson 02, you looked at truth tables. You
fed `1 1` into the inputs and `0` came out. Every single time. In the game there is
no such thing as a gate "being wrong".

A real gate being correct depends on two things: **its supply being clean** and
**its clock arriving on time**. If either is disturbed for an instant, the gate
still produces a result — but that result is no longer guaranteed to be right.

---

## What One Instant Is Enough For

Changing the state of a transistor takes time. When the clock signal arrives, a
flip-flop captures whatever is at its input at that moment. Normally the value has
settled by the time the signal comes.

```
normal:     computation ends ────────► value settles ──► clock captures   ✔ correct
glitch:     computation ends ─────  ✂  clock arrives EARLY ─────────────► ✘ a half-settled value was captured
```

There are two ways to do it, and both lead to the same door:

| Method | What is done |
|---|---|
| **Voltage glitch** | The supply voltage is dropped for a very short moment; gates slow down and the value cannot settle in time |
| **Clock glitch** | The clock pulse is sent earlier than normal; the circuit is sampled before it has finished computing |

MITRE calls these **fault attacks**: the goal is not to read a secret, but to make
**the circuit operate incorrectly.**

---

## It Corrupts the Decision, Not the Calculation

This is the heart of it. Corrupting a single bit in the middle of a calculation is
usually useless — the result becomes garbage and the program crashes.

What is valuable is the disturbance landing **exactly on top of a decision**:

```
is the signature valid?   →  no   →  halt
                              ↑
                    if the glitch lands RIGHT HERE
                              ↓
is the signature valid?   →  yes  →  carry on
```

In [lesson 11](../salterden_bilgisayara/11_selector_switch.md) you saw a decision
turn into a wire. That wire is a physical wire too; shake the value on it for an
instant and the machine reads "yes" where it should read "no".

The neighbouring number **CWE-1332** names one specific version of this: the glitch
causing an **instruction to be skipped**. If the skipped instruction was a check,
the check simply never happened.

## Real Cases

Two examples recorded on MITRE's CWE-1247 page:

| Record | What happened |
|---|---|
| **CVE-2019-17391** | With no protection against glitches, secure boot is bypassed and protected eFuses become readable |
| **CVE-2021-33478** | In the firmware of a communications device, particular pulses make the boot shell reachable |

The best-known example, though, is a games console: **the Xbox 360, 2011.** While
booting, the console compared the hash of the code it was about to load against the
value it expected. Researchers applied a pulse of roughly **20 nanoseconds** to the
processor's reset line at exactly that comparison; the comparison returned "no
difference" and modified code ran. The attack is known as the *Reset Glitch Hack*,
and it worked with roughly a one-in-four chance on each boot — that is, within a
few attempts.

> 🔑 A one-in-four chance is not a weakness of the attack here, it is **enough**.
> If an attacker can power the device on and off as often as they like, it does not
> need to work the first time. For someone with physical access, "sometimes works"
> and "works" are the same thing.

---

## How It Is Prevented

The three directions MITRE lists:

- **Detection at the circuit level.** Tunable Replica Circuits (TRC) or Razor-type
  flip-flops: they measure whether the value settled in time and raise a flag when
  it did not.
- **Platform sensors.** Circuits that watch for sudden disturbances on the supply
  and the clock; when a glitch is seen, the device resets or locks itself.
- **Redundancy on critical checks.** Ask the security question **twice**,
  preferably in different forms. It is far harder for a single pulse to land on
  both.

> 💡 The third is the only defence that can be done in software, which is why it
> shows up often in secure boot code: the same signature check run twice in a row,
> with a random delay in between.

Note: like [CWE-1300](./cwe_1300.md), these are **hardware** measures. They cannot
be added by a software update after the device has been manufactured — redundancy
on critical checks is a partial exception.

## Summary — Keep in Mind

```
☐ The game's second silent assumption: a gate always gives the right answer. In reality it does not.
☐ A gate's correctness depends on its supply and its clock; disturb either for an instant and a half-settled value is captured.
☐ Two methods, one outcome: drop the voltage for a moment, or send the clock early.
☐ The goal is not to read a secret, but to make the circuit operate INCORRECTLY (a fault attack).
☐ What is valuable is the disturbance landing on a DECISION: "no" → "yes".
☐ The neighbouring number CWE-1332: when the glitch causes an instruction to be skipped.
☐ Xbox 360 (2011): a ~20 ns pulse on the reset line at the moment of the hash comparison.
☐ A one-in-four chance is enough — someone with physical access simply tries again.
☐ Defences: TRC/Razor flip-flops, platform sensors, running a critical check twice.
```
